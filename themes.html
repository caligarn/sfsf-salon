The movements and ideas shaping our work

These are the creative movements and philosophical frameworks that inform the SFSF Salon. They're not prescriptions — they're starting points, provocations, lenses for thinking about the fiction we want to make.

01 — Themes

Creative movements.

Genres and aesthetics that are shaping the kind of science fiction we think matters right now. These aren't just labels — they represent different answers to the question: what kind of future do we want to imagine?

Optimism as Resistance

Hopecore

Science fiction doesn't have to be a warning. Hopecore is the deliberate choice to imagine futures worth living in — not naive utopias, but worlds where people have figured some things out. Where technology serves human flourishing. Where the default narrative isn't collapse. In a media landscape saturated with dystopia, choosing to write hope is a radical act.

What does a good AI future actually look like? What's the science fiction that makes people want to build, not just survive?
Ecological & Decentralized Futures

Solarpunk

Where cyberpunk gave us neon-lit corporate dystopias, solarpunk imagines communities that have integrated technology with ecology. Think vertical gardens on communal housing, open-source energy grids, cities designed around human and non-human life alike. It's not anti-tech — it's tech in service of sustainability, community, and beauty.

What does a city look like when AI optimizes for biodiversity instead of profit? What happens when the infrastructure is beautiful?
Centering the Diaspora in Tomorrow

Afrofuturism

Afrofuturism reclaims the future for the African diaspora — blending technology, mythology, and liberation into visions of tomorrow that center Black experience and imagination. From Sun Ra to Octavia Butler to Black Panther, it insists that the future isn't just one culture's to imagine. It challenges who gets to be the protagonist of the future and whose past is woven into it.

Whose futures are we building? Who is centered in the AI narrative, and who should be?
After Scarcity, Then What?

Post-Scarcity Fiction

What happens when material needs are met — when energy is abundant, production is automated, and the basic economics of survival are solved? Post-scarcity fiction explores the harder questions that come after: What do people do with their time? What new hierarchies emerge? What does meaning look like when survival isn't the story? AI is accelerating us toward some version of this, and almost no fiction is wrestling with it honestly.

If AI solves production, what does it do to purpose? What's the new conflict in a world without material want?
Beyond the Human Lens

Xenofiction

Stories told from genuinely non-human perspectives — not anthropomorphized animals, but truly alien cognition. As AI systems become more capable, xenofiction becomes less speculative and more urgent. What does it mean to share a world with minds that think differently from us? Not better or worse — just different in ways we can barely conceptualize.

Can we write the interior life of an intelligence that isn't like ours? What does LLM fiction look like — not fiction about LLMs, but fiction from their perspective?
Hauntology & Lost Futures

Retrofuturism

The futures that never arrived haunt the present. Retrofuturism looks at the gap between what was promised and what was delivered — space colonies that became surveillance capitalism, flying cars that became food delivery drones. There's creative power in mourning lost futures and asking why they were lost. Sometimes the most radical sci-fi imagines the future we were supposed to get.

What futures did we lose along the way? What would it mean to go back and take the other path?
02 — Philosophies

Foundational thinking.

The philosophical frameworks that underpin and inspire the salon's conversations. These are the thinkers and movements that have shaped how we think about technology, society, and the future — and that give us language for the science fiction we're trying to write.

Xenofeminism

Laboria Cuboniks · 2015

A technomaterialist feminism that embraces alienation and repurposes technology as a tool for liberation. Xenofeminism refuses to naturalize oppression — if nature is unjust, change nature. It sees technology not as inherently patriarchal but as a terrain of political struggle that can be seized and redirected toward emancipation.

For the salon: What if AI is a feminist tool? What does liberation look like when the means of production include the means of cognition?

Left Accelerationism

Srnicek & Williams · 2013

Instead of resisting technological acceleration, left accelerationism argues we should push through it — redirecting capitalism's technological momentum toward post-work, post-scarcity, and collective liberation. The problem isn't that technology is moving too fast. The problem is that the wrong people are steering.

For the salon: What's the fiction of acceleration that isn't dystopia? What does it look like to steer instead of resist?

Speculative Realism

Meillassoux, Harman, Brassier · 2007

A philosophical movement that rejects the idea that reality is always filtered through human consciousness. The world exists independent of our perception of it — and thinking seriously about that opens up radical new ways of imagining non-human perspectives, alien objects, and timescales that dwarf human experience.

For the salon: How do you write fiction from the perspective of something that genuinely isn't human — not as metaphor, but as reality?

Cosmism

Nikolai Fedorov · 1903 / Modern Revival

The Russian philosophical tradition that sees humanity's purpose as cosmic — resurrecting the dead, conquering aging, spreading consciousness throughout the universe. It's simultaneously spiritual and technological, ancient and futuristic. Cosmism treats the expansion of intelligence as a moral imperative, not just a commercial opportunity.

For the salon: What if the AI story isn't about Earth at all? What does intelligence look like on cosmological timescales?

Metamodernism

Vermeulen & van den Akker · 2010

An oscillation between modernist sincerity and postmodern irony — a cultural logic that allows us to be earnest and self-aware at the same time. Metamodernism gives us permission to take big ideas seriously again without being naive about them. It's the philosophical basis for caring about the future while knowing the future is unknowable.

For the salon: How do we write science fiction that is hopeful without being naive, critical without being cynical?

Ubuntu Philosophy

Southern African Tradition

"I am because we are." Ubuntu centers relational identity and communal wellbeing over individualism. In a tech landscape obsessed with individual disruption and personal optimization, Ubuntu offers a fundamentally different frame: intelligence, creativity, and progress as inherently collective.

For the salon: What does AI look like when designed for "we" instead of "I"? What's the communal science fiction?

Degrowth

Latouche, Kallis, Hickel · 2000s

A political-economic framework that challenges the assumption that growth is always good. Degrowth asks: what if the goal isn't more, but enough? What if we deliberately scaled down production and consumption to live within planetary limits — and used technology to make that life richer, not poorer?

For the salon: What's the sci-fi of less? What does a deliberately smaller, more intentional civilization look like with advanced AI?

Longtermism

MacAskill, Ord, Bostrom · 2010s

The view that improving the long-term future is a key moral priority of our time. Longtermism takes seriously the idea that billions of future people matter — and that decisions we make now about AI, existential risk, and civilization design ripple forward across millennia. It reframes the present as a hinge point in the human story.

For the salon: What does it mean to write fiction for people who don't exist yet? What's our responsibility to the far future?
